What Building an OT/ICS Vulnerability Management Program Actually Taught Me

A vulnerability management program built for an industrial control system environment — asset inventory, scan scoping by Purdue level, a stakeholder-negotiated policy, and six rounds of PowerShell remediations.

Where I Started

I came into this with an enterprise IT mental model. Scan the hosts, triage the findings, patch the criticals, close the tickets. That loop made sense to me. I had run it before.

What I did not understand going in was that this model collapses almost immediately when you apply it to an operational technology environment. Not because OT is exotic or especially complicated, but because the assumptions underneath the model are wrong. I had to find each of those assumptions and pull it out before I could build something that would actually work.

This is an account of what I found.

The Inversion I Did Not Expect

The first thing I had to learn was that OT inverts the CIA triad. Confidentiality, which sits at the top of almost every IT security framework, falls to third in an industrial environment. Availability comes first. Integrity comes second.

I understood this as a concept before I started. I did not understand what it meant in practice until I tried to write a remediation SLA.

My first draft said 30 days for critical findings. That number came from my IT background. It is a reasonable number for a web server. For a Level 2 HMI running legacy vendor software, it is nonsense. The vendor certification process alone can take 90 days. You cannot patch without it. A 30-day SLA on a device that cannot be patched in 30 days is not a policy. It is a compliance debt machine.

I rewrote the SLA to reflect what was actually possible. That was the first time I understood the inversion as a practical constraint rather than a framework principle.

The Purdue Model as a Scanning Decision

I had read about the Purdue Reference Model before this project. Levels 0 through 4, physical process up to enterprise network. It was an abstraction.

What I learned from building the scan framework is that the model is actually a set of scanning decisions. Each level has a different answer to the question “can I run a credentialed Nessus scan against this device?”

For Level 0 and Level 1, the answer is no. Not “no, but if you’re careful.” Just no. ICS-CERT has documented cases where active scanning caused process upsets on PLCs. The device’s connection table fills, a fault triggers, and the process goes offline. Passive monitoring tools like Claroty or Dragos exist specifically because active scanning is too dangerous at these levels.

I had to build this into the program architecture before the first scan ran. The scope was deliberately limited to Level 2 and Level 3 hosts. That felt like a constraint at first. Later I understood it was the only responsible starting point.

The Asset Inventory Problem

I started building the scan configuration before I finished the asset inventory. This was a mistake I caught before it caused a problem, but only barely.

The issue is that a vulnerability finding without asset context is just a number. Tenable returns a CVSS score. The CVSS score tells you how bad the vulnerability is in the abstract. It tells you nothing about whether the device runs a critical process or a building HVAC controller, whether it has a vendor certification requirement, or whether patching it requires a four-hour maintenance window and a field engineer.

I had to stop and complete the inventory before I could write the prioritization model. The inventory in assets/ot-asset-inventory.md covers every simulated device: its Purdue level, IP address, OS, function, vendor, patch constraint, and criticality. Every downstream decision in the program references that document.

The lesson was simple: you cannot score findings in context until you know the context. The inventory comes before everything else.

What I Learned from the Policy Process

My first policy draft was technically coherent and operationally useless. It had the right structure. It had the right categories. The people who would actually have to live by it would not have signed it.

The operations manager’s first question was whether the scan could disrupt production. I did not have a specific answer ready. I had a general answer: “we’ll use a maintenance window.” He needed the exact IP addresses to be scanned, the credential type, the scan window times, and a written abort procedure if process alarms triggered. Not because he was being difficult. Because those were the actual questions the scan raised for someone responsible for production continuity.

The OT engineer’s question was about the SCADA historian and SMB authentication. I had planned a full credentialed scan. He told me the historian could not accept a domain account without a configuration change that required vendor approval. I had not accounted for this. The two-pass approach came from that conversation: a passive asset profile first, then the credentialed scan after the vendor-approved change.

The safety officer’s question caught me most off-guard. She asked who would review changes to safety-instrumented systems. I had not treated SIS differently from standard Level 2 devices in my draft. That was wrong, and she was right to push back. I added a mandatory safety officer review step for any remediation touching a SIS. The program is better for it.

None of these issues showed up in the policy text. They showed up in conversation. The meeting notes in meeting-notes/ exist because those conversations are where the real program gets built.

The Credential Problem

I had used Tenable against Windows hosts before and understood the general shape of credentialed scanning. What I had not worked through carefully was the local account authentication behavior.

The LocalAccountTokenFilterPolicy registry key was new territory for me. Without it set to 1, a non-RID-500 local administrator account authenticates over the network with a filtered token, which means Tenable receives an Access Denied on most of the interesting scan targets. The finding list comes back incomplete in ways that are hard to diagnose because the scan reports as authenticated. The actual authenticated results are just quietly wrong.

Setting that key and understanding why it was necessary was a piece of Windows internals I had not engaged with before. It also crystallized why just-in-time credentials matter: the scan account has elevated network access during the scan window that should not persist after the scan ends. Create the account before, delete it after.

What the Injection Script Showed Me

I wrote the injection script to simulate a legacy OT HMI. Each configuration change represents something I had read about in threat advisories: SMBv1 for EternalBlue, NLA disabled for pre-authentication RDP exposure, LmCompatibilityLevel 0 for legacy device compatibility, the shared scada-operator account that never gets rotated, USB autorun for removable media propagation.

What surprised me was how normal each of these looked in isolation. None of them are the result of negligence. SMBv1 stays on because the HMI vendor’s communication stack depends on it. NLA gets disabled because an old application breaks without the workaround. The shared account exists because multiple operators need emergency access and rotating it means touching twenty clipboards. Autorun stays enabled because operators bring configuration files on drives.

These are operational decisions made by people trying to keep the process running. The vulnerabilities are a side effect of those decisions, not their goal. Writing the script helped me understand why OT hardening is harder than enterprise hardening: the people who created these configurations had legitimate reasons.

The Prioritization Gap

The raw Tenable output does not know what environment it ran against. A critical finding on a PLC Ethernet adapter gets the same red as a critical finding on a Level 3 historian. In enterprise IT, that is probably fine. In OT, it produces a completely wrong picture of what needs attention first.

I built the 4-dimension scoring model to close this gap. CVSS contributes 30 percent. Operational impact contributes 30 percent. Zone proximity contributes 25 percent. OT exploitability context contributes 15 percent. The weights feel approximately right for this environment; I would not claim they are universal.

The LmCompatibilityLevel finding was where the model proved its value. There is no CVE for this. In a pure CVSS-driven program, it would fall to low priority by default. The OT model assigns it high operational impact because a compromised OT account gives process control access, and high zone proximity because the device is at Level 2 with visibility into Level 1. The finding ends up in the high priority tier despite having no assigned CVSS score.

That result surprised me a little. It also felt right once I thought through what the finding actually meant in this environment.

The CAB Process

Going into the CAB meeting, I thought of it as a gate I had to get through. I came out of it understanding it differently.

The operations manager conditioned Round 2 on a documented breakglass procedure. He was not being obstructive. He was asking a reasonable question: if we remove the shared admin account, what happens during an emergency? That question had not occurred to me when I scoped the remediation. The condition made the change safer.

The OT engineer conditioned Round 3 on a protocol audit. He needed confirmation that no Level 1 device was polling the HMI over SMBv1 before the protocol got disabled. That was also a question I should have asked before I scoped the round. The condition was the right call.

Round 6 was where I had to stop and think the hardest. LmCompatibilityLevel could not be fixed. Three legacy devices on the Level 1 subnet authenticate using LM. Raising the setting would break those connections. The CAB approved Tier 3: compensating controls in place, documented, scheduled for remediation when the Level 1 devices are replaced in Q2 2027.

I had not fully internalized what Tier 3 meant until I had to write the compensating controls register entry for it. It is not a workaround and it is not a failure. It is the honest acknowledgment that the correct fix is not currently available, that everything available has been applied, and that the residual risk is tracked with a defined exit condition. Writing that entry was the clearest moment in the whole project.

What the Remediation Rounds Showed Me

Most of the remediation rounds went cleanly. The scripts in scripts/remediations/ each follow the same pattern: read the current state, make the change, verify the new state. The before/after verification mattered more than I expected, not because the changes were complex but because having the evidence record makes the work real. A change ticket that says “disabled SMBv1” is less convincing than one that appends PowerShell output showing EnableSMB1Protocol : False.

Round 6 was different because the script does not change the setting it was tasked with. I had to think carefully about what the script was actually doing: re-enabling the firewall with OT-subnet-scoped rules, enabling NTLM auditing to log every LM authentication event, documenting the constraint. The script exists not to fix the finding but to reduce the attack surface around it and create a paper trail.

Writing that script clarified something about compensating controls. The goal is not to pretend the risk is gone. The goal is to make exploitation harder and detection more likely while you wait for the actual fix to become viable.

What I Would Do Differently

The asset inventory should have been the first artifact, not the third. I built it after the policy and before the scan, which was better than building it after the scan, but the policy was weaker for not having the inventory as its foundation. Zone classifications and criticality ratings belong in the policy before the policy goes out for stakeholder review.

The scan authorization document needed to be more specific about the abort procedure earlier. I added specific process alarm thresholds at the operations manager’s request during the stakeholder meeting. Those thresholds should have been in the initial draft. Asking someone to sign an authorization with a vague abort procedure is asking them to take on risk you have not quantified.

And I should have asked the OT engineer about SMBv1 dependencies on the subnet before I scoped Round 3. The protocol audit was the right step; I just should have initiated it rather than waiting for the CAB to surface it.

What the Program Is Actually For

This was a learning exercise run against a simulated environment. The Azure VMs are not production PLCs. The injected vulnerabilities are representative but not the full picture of what an actual OT assessment would find.

What the program demonstrates is the shape of the decision-making in this domain: where scanning needs authorization before it can proceed, where the remediation is not “fix it,” where the finding you document rather than patch is the most important entry in the whole register.

The compensating controls register, the meeting notes, the CAB approvals, the before/after verification outputs: these are not compliance theater. They are how you show your work in an environment where a change gone wrong takes a production line offline. In enterprise IT, the security team owns the patch window. In OT, the security team is a guest in someone else’s process. Understanding that distinction is most of what I learned.
