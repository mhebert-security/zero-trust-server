IEC 62443 Without the Jargon: What It Actually Requires

Published: 2026-10-01 · Module: Module 4, Article 8: Standards and Compliance

IEC 62443 and NERC CIP are the two standards that govern industrial security, and both are usually taught as jargon. This module translates them into controls.

Before I touch the controls, I need to explain the structure, because IEC 62443 is not a document. It is a family of documents organized into four series. You cannot read "IEC 62443." You read IEC 62443-3-3, or 2-1, or 4-2, depending on whether you are designing a system, running a program, or procuring a component. This distinction matters because assessments often misidentify which part of the standard applies to a given question.

Series 1 covers terminology and concepts. It is reference material.

Series 2 covers the security management system: how organizations should run their industrial security programs (2-1), manage patches (2-3), and define requirements for service providers (2-4).

Series 3 covers system-level requirements: risk assessment methodology (3-2) and the actual security requirements for a system (3-3). This is where most of the technical work lives.

Series 4 covers product-level requirements: secure development lifecycle (4-1) and component requirements (4-2). Vendors use these. Asset owners use them when evaluating what to procure.

When a customer tells you they need to "comply with IEC 62443," the first question is which parts, at which security level, for which systems. Without that scoping the work is open-ended.

The Zone and Conduit Model

IEC 62443 does not tell you to put a firewall between IT and OT and call it done. It gives you a framework for segmenting your environment into zones and managing communication between them through conduits.

A zone is a grouping of assets that share a common security level requirement. The standard does not dictate how you draw the boundaries. You draw them based on consequence of compromise, operational dependencies, and regulatory requirements. A historian server and an HMI might be in the same zone. A safety instrumented system should almost certainly be in its own zone.

A conduit is the means by which data moves between zones. A conduit is not just a firewall rule. It is the firewall, the protocol, the data flow direction, the authentication mechanism, and the monitoring that governs that communication path. You document conduits. You justify them. Every unmonitored connection between zones is an undocumented conduit, which is a finding.

The practical output of zone and conduit modeling is a network architecture document that maps assets to zones, zones to security level targets, and conduits to the controls that enforce them. This document becomes the primary artifact for any IEC 62443 assessment.

Security Levels 1 through 4

Security Levels describe the threat actor capability that a zone's controls are designed to defeat. They are not a maturity rating. SL 4 does not mean "very mature." It means the controls in that zone are designed to resist a sophisticated, well-resourced adversary with insider knowledge.

SL 1 protects against casual or unintentional violations. An employee who misconfigures a device. A USB drive plugged into the wrong port. The controls at this level are basic: password policy, physical access logging, network segmentation.

SL 2 protects against an intentional attacker using simple, publicly available tools without insider knowledge. A script kiddie running Shodan and Metasploit. Controls include multi-factor authentication for remote access, encrypted communications, and role-based access control.

SL 3 protects against a motivated attacker with ICS-specific knowledge using sophisticated means. The adversary understands Modbus, DNP3, and PROFINET. They are patient. Controls at this level include defense in depth across all network segments, anomaly detection tuned to OT protocols, and strict application allowlisting on engineering workstations.

SL 4 protects against a nation-state-level actor with insider knowledge, significant resources, and the ability to conduct a sustained campaign. The controls here are extensive: hardware-based authentication, cryptographic verification of firmware, physical tamper detection, and continuous behavioral monitoring. SL 4 is rare. Power generation critical infrastructure and defense-related manufacturing are the domains where it appears.

The standard distinguishes between the Target SL, what you want to achieve, and the Achieved SL, what your current controls actually provide. The gap between them is your remediation backlog.

The Seven Foundational Requirements

IEC 62443-3-3 organizes all system-level security requirements into seven Foundational Requirements. Every control in the standard maps to one of them. Once you understand the FRs, the rest of the standard is detail.

FR 1: Identification and Authentication Control. Every human user and every device that connects to the system must be identified and authenticated before access is granted. This includes HMIs, engineering workstations, historian servers, and field devices where the technology supports it. The strength of authentication required scales with the security level.

FR 2: Use Control. Authenticated users and devices get only the access they need for their role. This is least-privilege applied to OT. An operator account should not be able to modify PLC logic. A remote support session should not have write access to a safety system. Use control also covers the ability to enforce policies during sessions and to audit what was done.

FR 3: System Integrity. The system should detect and protect against unauthorized changes. This covers firmware integrity verification, protection against malicious code, and ensuring that communication between components has not been tampered with. In practice this means application allowlisting on endpoints and integrity checking on field device firmware where vendor support exists.

FR 4: Data Confidentiality. Sensitive information moving across conduits or stored on devices should be protected from unauthorized disclosure. In OT this is more nuanced than in IT. Process data itself often has low confidentiality requirements but high integrity and availability requirements. The focus tends to be on protecting configuration data, credentials, and any data that crosses into IT systems.

FR 5: Restricted Data Flow. Data should flow between zones only through documented conduits and only in the direction and protocol the conduit permits. This is the firewall and DMZ requirement made explicit. Unidirectional gateways are the strongest implementation for zones that require data to leave but never enter.

FR 6: Timely Response to Events. The system should detect, log, and respond to security-relevant events in a timeframe appropriate to the consequence. This is where your SIEM integration, your OT-specific detection rules, and your incident response procedures connect back to the standard. Without logging there is no timely response. Without detection rules tuned to ICS protocols the logs are noise.

FR 7: Resource Availability. The system should remain available under attack. Denial of service protection, redundant communication paths, and graceful degradation modes all fall here. In OT the consequence of unavailability is often physical: a plant that shuts down unexpectedly may be harder to restart safely than to keep running.

Translating This into Controls

The standard does not prescribe specific products. It prescribes requirements at each security level. Your job is to select controls that satisfy those requirements for your target SL.

For SL 2, a minimum viable control set looks like this: network segmentation between the control network and any business network enforced by a stateful firewall with default-deny rules; MFA for any remote access path into the control network; role-based accounts on HMIs and engineering workstations with no shared credentials; time-synchronized logging from all network-capable devices to a central collector; and a patch management process that documents approved patches and the timeline for testing and deploying them to OT assets.

For SL 3, add anomaly detection tuned to OT protocols running on a dedicated passive sensor, application allowlisting on engineering workstations with a formal approval process for new applications, encrypted communication on all conduits that cross zone boundaries where the field devices support it, and a formal security risk assessment per IEC 62443-3-2 that documents your zone and conduit decisions.

The assessment process under 3-2 is not a checklist. It is a structured risk analysis: identify assets, assign them to zones, determine the target SL for each zone based on consequence analysis, assess the current controls, identify the gap to the target SL, and prioritize remediation. The output is the zone and conduit model with SL targets documented and justified.

What I Have Not Solved Yet

NERC CIP is not in this article. The two standards overlap in the electric utility sector but are structured differently. NERC CIP is prescriptive and enforceable with penalties. IEC 62443 is a framework. Mapping one to the other is its own dedicated exercise and gets its own article.

I have not worked through how IEC 62443-4-2 applies to procurement in practice. When an asset owner specifies SL 2 for a component, what exactly should they look for in a vendor's documentation to verify that claim? The 4-2 requirements are detailed, and vendor compliance is inconsistently documented.

The gap between "we are compliant" and "our controls actually work" is where the detection engineering from the earlier modules becomes relevant. Compliance demonstrates that controls exist. Detection validates that they function. I plan to tie this together in the assessment lab in the companion project.
