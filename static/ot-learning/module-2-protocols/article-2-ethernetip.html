EtherNet/IP and CIP: How Rockwell PLCs Talk and How Attackers Listen

Published: 2026-09-18 · Module: Module 2, Article 2 · Protocol Literacy · Code: github.com/mhebert-security/enip-lab

I came into EtherNet/IP expecting it to be Modbus with a fancier name. I was wrong in a way that took me a while to fully appreciate.

Modbus tells you a device exists on port 502 and then it stops talking. You send a read, and if the address is right, you get a value back. No name. No vendor. No firmware version. No clue what the device is unless someone told you out of band. I spent real time on a Modbus segment once trying to figure out what was on it, and it was slow. Address scanning, guessing register ranges, cross-referencing against documentation that was partly wrong. It felt like archaeology.

EtherNet/IP is not that. The first time I watched a List Identity response come back on the wire I had to read it twice. Vendor name. Device family. Firmware revision. Serial number. Product name as a human-readable ASCII string. All of it, handed back to whoever asked, with no credentials required. I sent a 24-byte packet and got back a labeled diagram of the device.

That reaction, where I went "wait, it just tells you?" is what this article is about. Not just what EtherNet/IP is, but what it means that it works the way it does, and why I think understanding that gap between Modbus and EtherNet/IP is one of the more useful things someone coming from IT security can internalize about OT.

What EtherNet/IP and CIP Actually Are

The name tripped me up for longer than I want to admit. EtherNet/IP does not mean Ethernet over IP or Ethernet and IP. It stands for EtherNet Industrial Protocol, and the actual protocol underneath it is CIP, the Common Industrial Protocol, which is older than EtherNet/IP by about a decade.

Here is the history that clicked things into place for me. CIP started as the application layer for DeviceNet, which was a fieldbus Rockwell (then Allen-Bradley) built on CAN bus in the early 1990s. When they extended it to ControlNet, a deterministic coax-based network for motion and inter-controller traffic, they kept the same application layer. When Ethernet became viable for industrial environments in the late 1990s, they wrapped that same CIP application layer in TCP and UDP and called it EtherNet/IP. First release was 2001. The standards body they founded to govern it is ODVA, Open DeviceNet Vendors Association.

So CIP is the thing that actually does the work, and it has been running on different physical layers for thirty years. DeviceNet is CIP over CAN. ControlNet is CIP over coaxial or fiber. EtherNet/IP is CIP over TCP and UDP. The application layer does not care which physical layer carried it, which is why a tag name in Rockwell's symbolic addressing looks identical whether it is going through a DeviceNet sensor or coming out of a ControlLogix on Ethernet.

The standard is published by ODVA. Rockwell is the dominant implementer by a wide margin and is what most people mean when they say EtherNet/IP, but Omron, Cognex, some Siemens cards, and various motion control vendors implement it too. When I say Rockwell in this article I mean the flagship implementation. The discovery and introspection behavior I am describing is protocol-level, not a Rockwell quirk.

The three ports I memorized first:

TCP 44818 carries explicit messaging. Request and response, connection-oriented. This is where reads, writes, program uploads, firmware downloads, and object dictionary queries live. The interesting operations.

UDP 44818 carries List Identity and List Services broadcasts. These are the discovery mechanisms. This is the port that surprised me.

UDP 2222 carries implicit messaging. In CIP, implicit means produced and consumed on a schedule without a request. A ControlLogix and an I/O block exchange data on this port at a configured rate, often in the tens of milliseconds, and the traffic looks nothing like request-response. It is a multicast group in many configurations and it just flows continuously. This is the plane where actual process control happens.

Most of the attack surface lives on TCP 44818. Most of the reconnaissance value lives on both UDP ports. That distinction matters and I will come back to it.

Two Transports, Two Problems

The split between explicit and implicit messaging is the thing I found hardest to build intuition for coming from IT. There is no clean analogy. In IT you have request-response and streaming. In EtherNet/IP you have request-response and a second pattern that has been made stateless and periodic because the underlying process runs on a schedule that the network cannot be allowed to slow down.

Explicit messaging I understood immediately. Send a request to TCP 44818, get back a value, close the connection. It looks like an API call. An attacker reading tags is doing exactly that.

Implicit messaging took me longer. Every PLC has a configured set of I/O connections, and each one has a fixed multicast group, a fixed exchange rate, and a payload size that corresponds to the bytes that connection carries. Watch a segment for five minutes on UDP 2222 and you have a map of which devices are talking to which controllers, how often, and how much data each connection carries, without ever sending a packet yourself. Passively. Which means no IDS that works on auth failures or malformed requests sees you, because you have done nothing.

The other thing I noticed is that implicit traffic on 2222 is often invisible to IT security tooling. It is UDP, it is on a nonstandard port, it is multicast, and on some networks it is extremely high volume. If a plant has monitoring at all, it is often monitoring TCP. 2222 goes unlogged.

The CIP Object Model

CIP is object-oriented in a way that reflects its age. Every device exposes a set of objects. Every object is an instance of a class. Every class has attributes that can be read or written depending on the class and the service. It is structured and navigable in a way that Modbus is not, which has real consequences.

The classes I kept running into:

Identity Object (Class 0x01) is what the device says about itself. Vendor ID, device type, product code, revision, status, serial number, product name. Every EtherNet/IP device has one, and it is designed to be readable by anyone on the network. This is the thing that answered my 24-byte question with a labeled reply.

Message Router (Class 0x02) is the internal dispatcher that routes CIP messages to the correct object. Usually transparent to an outside observer but worth knowing exists.

Assembly Object (Class 0x04) defines the byte layout of an implicit I/O connection. The implicit traffic on UDP 2222 is carrying these. Knowing the assembly layout is how you interpret what you see on that port.

Connection Manager (Class 0x06) makes and breaks connections. Both implicit I/O connections and explicit CIP connections go through here.

Symbol Object (Class 0x6B) is Rockwell-specific and is the class that makes tag names work. This is the one that lets you ask a ControlLogix to read the tag named Cooling_Pump_Status and get an answer back. It is also the one that lets you enumerate every tag name the controller is running.

The object model is extensible by design. Rockwell uses higher class numbers for vendor-specific extensions. Other vendors do the same. This is how the protocol stays vendor-agnostic at the standard level while letting implementations go deeper.

What a Packet Looks Like

I found it useful to look at the bytes early. The encapsulation header is 24 bytes and is the same for all EtherNet/IP messages over TCP and UDP:

Command         2 bytes
Length          2 bytes
Session Handle  4 bytes
Status          4 bytes
Sender Context  8 bytes
Options         4 bytes

For a List Identity, Command is 0x0063, Length is zero, and everything else is zero or the sender's own identifier. That is the entire request. 24 bytes. No credentials anywhere in it.

The reply carries a CIP Identity Object:

Vendor ID           2 bytes
Device Type         2 bytes
Product Code        2 bytes
Revision            2 bytes (major and minor)
Status              2 bytes
Serial Number       4 bytes
Product Name        length-prefixed string
State               1 byte

When I ran this in my lab I got back something that decoded to: Vendor 0x0001 (Allen-Bradley/Rockwell), Device Type 0x000E (Programmable Logic Controller), Revision 32.11, Serial number (a test value), Product Name "1756-L83E/B LOGIX5583", State 0x03 (operational).

All of that from 24 bytes and no authentication. Shodan has been doing this to every reachable IPv4 address for years and has indexed hundreds of thousands of industrial devices. It is not doing anything clever. It is asking politely and recording who answers.

Reading Tags by Name

This is the part I want to spend the most time on because it is where EtherNet/IP becomes something qualitatively different from what came before.

Rockwell's engineering environment, Studio 5000, encourages engineers to give tags descriptive names. Motor_1_Run. Tank_Level_Setpoint. Valve_3_Command. Overpressure_Alarm. These names are for humans. When an engineer opens a rung of ladder logic in Studio 5000, the tag names are what they see. The difference between Tag_00041 and Cooling_Pump_Status is the difference between a program someone can maintain over a decade and one that turns into archaeology when the original author leaves.

Rockwell has been teaching this practice for twenty years. It is correct engineering. It is why Rockwell's ecosystem is trusted by engineers who have to keep these systems running across multiple plant turnarounds and staff generations.

Here is what that means on the wire. Symbolic addressing means that if you can talk CIP over TCP 44818, you can ask the controller to read the tag named Cooling_Pump_Status by name, and the controller will read it and send you the value. Read service is 0x4C, takes the tag name as payload. Write service is 0x4D, takes the tag name and a value. There is no authentication on either, in the base protocol.

More than that: you can enumerate the tag dictionary. You can ask the controller for a list of every tag it is running, with types and addresses. The process, named, in order, from the engineering environment.

I sat with this for a while when I first understood it. In Modbus, an attacker who gets on the network still has to figure out what coil 5 and register 10 mean before they can do anything useful with them. That takes time and guesswork and documentation they may or may not have. In EtherNet/IP, the controller tells them. The tag names are the process schematic delivered on request.

I am not saying this is a mistake on Rockwell's part. The tradeoff is real and it was made correctly for the environment the protocol was designed for, which was a closed plant network where every connected device was owned by the same operator. That environment is not what these systems are deployed into now. That is the problem.

CVE-2021-22681 and the Hardcoded Key

In February 2021, Claroty disclosed a vulnerability in Rockwell's Logix controllers. Studio 5000 and the controllers themselves used a hardcoded cryptographic key to establish what was supposed to be a trusted engineering session. The key was the same across every Logix controller family and every firmware version. It was also extractable from Studio 5000, which was not meaningfully obfuscated.

The consequence: anyone who could get a copy of Studio 5000, which is to say anyone who wanted to, could extract the key and talk to any Logix controller as a legitimate engineering workstation. The controllers would accept the session, and from there they would accept reads, writes, and firmware updates. CVSS 9.8. CVE-2021-22681.

The response from Rockwell is what I found most instructive. They initially pushed back, arguing the authentication mechanism was working as designed and the key was only in the legitimate engineering software. CISA published an advisory anyway and called it a vulnerability. The argument went public.

I spent time understanding Rockwell's position because I think it was sincerely held and not entirely wrong. The key was not leaked. It existed in the tool that was supposed to have it. The intended access model was Studio 5000 running on a workstation an authorized engineer controlled, on a network only that engineer could reach. From inside that model, the argument "the tool is the identity" is not crazy.

The problem is that "the tool is the identity" is not authentication in any meaningful sense when the tool is installed on tens of thousands of machines worldwide and the key is trivially extractable from any of them. Authentication requires that the authenticating secret be held by a specific, identifiable principal. When the secret is a shared constant present in all copies of the software, it is a fixed value, not a secret, and sessions established with it are not authenticated sessions. What Rockwell was calling an authentication mechanism was really a handshake ritual. Rituals can be learned by anyone.

What I took from this beyond the specific CVE: the design pattern it belongs to, treating possession of the vendor's software as proof of authorization, is not a Rockwell invention. It shows up repeatedly across the industrial sector. CVE-2021-22681 is one named instance of it. MITRE's T0891 Hardcoded Credentials is the class it belongs to, and the class is large.

The Attacker Listens

Here is the thing that I think is actually the most important takeaway from everything above.

The reason EtherNet/IP has more published attack surface than Modbus is not that the protocol is more broken. It is that the protocol produces more intelligence, and it does so before the attacker has done anything active.

A passive tap on a Rockwell segment, no scanning, just listening:

List Identity broadcasts. Many EtherNet/IP devices broadcast their presence at regular intervals without anyone asking. Twenty minutes of capture gives an attacker a device inventory with vendor, family, firmware version, and serial number for every EtherNet/IP device on the segment. No packets sent to any target.

Implicit I/O traffic on UDP 2222. The I/O connections between controllers and I/O blocks are all visible. From the multicast groups and payload sizes, you can infer which controller owns which process area, which I/O blocks are under which controller, and the update rates for each connection. That is a diagram of the physical layout of the process.

Explicit messaging on TCP 44818 from legitimate clients. HMIs polling tag values, engineering workstations connecting for programming sessions, historians collecting data. All of this is on the wire. The tag names in those reads are the exact names the attacker will need when they are ready to write something.

None of this requires active scanning. None of it trips an IDS that looks for scanning or malformed packets. An attacker with a laptop on the same VLAN, or a tap, or a route to the segment from a compromised machine on the corporate network, has all of this without having touched a single PLC.

The thing I keep returning to is that this is not a flaw the protocol accidentally acquired. It is a consequence of designing for interoperability on a trusted network. Every feature that made EtherNet/IP manageable at scale, self-description, symbolic addressing, discoverable topology, is also a feature that does reconnaissance work on the attacker's behalf when the network is no longer trusted. The protocol was not designed for the threat model it now operates under.

The ATT&CK Mapping and the Empty CVE Column

Running the same exercise I did for Modbus, mapping technique to CVE:

T0842 Network Sniffing. Passive capture of EtherNet/IP traffic. No CVE.

T0846 Remote System Discovery. Sending List Identity requests. Unauthenticated by design. No CVE.

T0801 Monitor Process State. Reading tags by name over explicit messaging. No CVE.

T0811 Data from Information Repositories. Enumerating the tag dictionary and object dictionary. No CVE.

T0845 Program Upload. Using read services to exfiltrate the controller's running program. No CVE.

T0855 Unauthorized Command Message. Writing tags. No CVE.

T0836 Modify Parameter. Writing setpoint tags. No CVE.

T0839 Module Firmware. Uploading firmware. CVE-2021-22681 is the named enabler on Logix controllers. The technique has been executed on other Rockwell families without a CVE attached.

T0891 Hardcoded Credentials. The class CVE-2021-22681 belongs to.

Compare this to the Modbus list where the CVE column was empty everywhere. Here it is empty almost everywhere, and the one entry that is not empty describes a single vendor's implementation of a design pattern the entire sector uses. The pattern is the same: the protocol layer is not where the CVEs live. The CVEs describe the narrow subset of failures a vendor got caught on publicly. The techniques that actually matter are architectural.

What to Do About It

Three things with a specific EtherNet/IP flavor.

Segment, and take UDP 2222 seriously. Every implicit I/O connection is a fixed multicast group and a fixed exchange rate. If you know what those are supposed to be, you can write an ACL that allows only the known flows. If you do not know what they are supposed to be, that is the finding. The implicit I/O plane should be one of the most legible things on a well-managed network, and in most facilities nobody has ever tried to enumerate it.

Monitor passively before you scan actively. A passive sensor on the OT segment will capture List Identity broadcasts, baseline explicit messaging flows, and build a device inventory without touching a single PLC. It will not produce log entries on the targets. It will not risk an unscheduled stop. If you are going to scan, do it, but know that an attacker who wants to avoid detection will not scan. They will listen. The protocol gives them enough to work with.

Use CIP Security where you can and be honest about where you cannot. CIP Security is the ODVA extension that wraps CIP in TLS and DTLS with certificate-based authentication. It has existed since around 2018. Adoption is low for the usual reason: the devices that most need it are the ones least able to run it, and a plant where a thirty-year-old controller is still making process decisions is not a plant that can roll out PKI to its I/O blocks. The new devices will get CIP Security. The new devices will be a small fraction of the install base for a long time.

One more thing worth saying out loud: the tag names on your controllers are a security-relevant artifact. Not because you should obfuscate them, which would be a bad operational tradeoff and would not stop an attacker who can read the wire traffic of a legitimate client anyway. But because the fact that they are readable to anyone who can reach the port should be part of your threat model. If your segmentation assumes nobody outside a small set of hosts can reach TCP 44818 on a Logix controller, then in the case where that assumption is wrong, the attacker does not just have access. They have a map.

What I Did Not Solve

Two things I kept running into that I do not have clean answers for.

The tag name problem has no real solution at the protocol level. Descriptive tag names are the only way to maintain a Rockwell system over decades. They are also the informational surface an attacker reads to understand the process. You cannot have a controller that tells legitimate clients what its tags are while hiding them from an attacker, because the protocol cannot distinguish the two. The only answer is to keep the attacker off the network. That answer puts all the weight on segmentation, and segmentation is the control that plant operators most commonly resist because they were told, sometimes correctly, that it would break something.

I do not know how common CVE-2021-22681 is as a pattern. The Rockwell case got a CVE and a public dispute between the vendor and CISA. I believe similar patterns exist in other vendors' products without the same visibility. The industrial sector repeatedly reaches for "possession of our software" as an authorization check. I do not have a good way to estimate how many products do this, and I do not trust any estimate I have seen. This feels like the kind of thing that has more named instances waiting.

Companion Project

The companion for this article is an active List Identity lab at github.com/mhebert-security/enip-lab. Two Python scripts, standard library only. One server that pretends to be a Rockwell ControlLogix on UDP 44818. One client that sends the 24-byte List Identity request and prints the full reply in hex. Run them together and you watch the exchange an attacker runs during discovery: a 24-byte question, a reply with vendor, family, firmware revision, serial number, and product name, no credentials at any point. Wireshark decodes both packets automatically on port 44818.
