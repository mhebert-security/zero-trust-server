EtherNet/IP and CIP: How Rockwell PLCs Talk and How Attackers Listen

Published: 2026-09-18 · Module: Module 2, Article 2 · Protocol Literacy · Code: github.com/mhebert-security/enip-lab

Modbus tells you that a device exists on port 502 and then it stops talking. You send a read, and if the address is right, you get a value. You get no name, no vendor, no firmware revision, no clue what the device is unless somebody has told you out of band. Getting oriented on an unfamiliar Modbus network is like walking into a dark room and feeling your way around the furniture.

EtherNet/IP is the opposite, and mostly on purpose. Ask a Rockwell PLC who it is and it will tell you. The vendor. The device family. The firmware revision. The serial number. The product name as a string a human can read. And if you ask the next question, it will also list the tag names it is running, which is the equivalent of handing a stranger a labeled schematic of the process it is controlling.

That is not a vulnerability. It is how the protocol was designed. It is how interoperability is supposed to work. It is also a description of how an attacker would design a reconnaissance phase if they had been invited to.

This article is about EtherNet/IP and the CIP protocol underneath it. How Rockwell PLCs talk, what makes that talk able to scale across a plant floor, and how the same features that made the protocol usable for a control engineer made it into a target list for an attacker with a laptop on the same network.

By the end I want the reader to walk away with one idea. In Modbus, the attacker has to know what to send. In EtherNet/IP, the device tells them.

What EtherNet/IP and CIP Actually Are

The name is unhelpful. EtherNet/IP does not mean "Ethernet over IP" or "Ethernet and IP." It stands for "EtherNet Industrial Protocol," and the protocol underneath the Ethernet is CIP, the Common Industrial Protocol, which predates EtherNet/IP by a decade.

CIP started life as the application layer for DeviceNet, a CAN based fieldbus that Rockwell (then Allen-Bradley) developed in the early 1990s. Rockwell extended the same application layer to ControlNet, a deterministic network for higher speed motion and inter-controller communication. When Ethernet started to become viable in industrial settings in the late 1990s, Rockwell (and the standards body it founded, ODVA, which stands for Open DeviceNet Vendors Association) wrapped the same CIP application layer in TCP and UDP and called it EtherNet/IP. The first release was 2001.

So CIP is the actual protocol, and it has been wearing different physical layers for thirty years. DeviceNet is CIP over CAN. ControlNet is CIP over coaxial or fiber. EtherNet/IP is CIP over TCP and UDP. The application layer does not care which physical layer carried it, which is why a tag name in Rockwell's symbolic addressing looks the same whether it is descending into a DeviceNet sensor or climbing out of a ControlLogix through Ethernet.

The standard is published by ODVA, not by Rockwell. Rockwell is the dominant implementer of EtherNet/IP by a wide margin, and it is the vendor most people mean when they say "EtherNet/IP PLC," but other vendors implement the protocol too: Omron, Cognex, some Siemens add on cards, various robot and motion control vendors. When I say Rockwell in this article I mean the flagship implementation. Everything I describe about the protocol applies to the standard, and the discovery and introspection behavior is not a Rockwell quirk.

The network ports are the first thing to memorize.

TCP port 44818 carries explicit messaging. Explicit messaging in CIP is request and response, connection oriented, and is where the interesting operations live: read a tag, write a tag, upload a program, download firmware, query the object dictionary, walk the identity object.

UDP port 44818 carries the List Identity and List Services broadcasts. These are the discovery mechanisms, and I will come back to them, because they are the reason an attacker with a passive network tap learns more about a Rockwell network than they would learn about almost any other industrial network.

UDP port 2222 carries implicit messaging. Implicit in CIP means "produced and consumed on a schedule without a request." A ControlLogix PLC and an I/O block exchange data on this port at a configured rate, typically in the tens of milliseconds, and the traffic looks nothing like the request and response pattern of explicit messaging. It is a multicast group in many configurations, and it just flows. This is the plane where the actual process control happens, implicitly, continuously, without any individual packet being a command.

Most of the attack surface lives on TCP 44818. Most of the reconnaissance surface lives on both UDP ports.

Two Transports, Two Problems

The split between explicit and implicit messaging is the first thing a newcomer from IT struggles with, because there is no clean analogy. In IT you have request and response (HTTP, SQL) and you have streaming (video, log shipping). In EtherNet/IP you have request and response and you have a second request and response pattern that has been made stateless and periodic because the underlying process has to update on a schedule the network cannot be allowed to slow down.

Explicit messaging is where an attacker goes when they want to read a tag. Send a request to TCP 44818, receive the value, close the connection. Standard, boring, easy to understand.

Implicit messaging is where an attacker goes when they want to see the shape of the process without doing anything that could be detected. Every PLC has a configured set of I/O connections, and every one of them has a fixed multicast group, a fixed exchange rate, and a payload size that corresponds to the number of bytes the connection carries. Watch a segment for five minutes on UDP 2222 and you have a diagram of which devices are talking to which controllers, how often, and how much data each connection carries. You have that without sending a single packet yourself. You have it passively, which means no IDS that works on auth failures or malformed requests will see you, because you have done nothing to be seen doing.

The last piece is that implicit traffic on 2222 is often not logged by IT security tools. It is UDP, it is on a nonstandard port, it is multicast, and it is high volume on some networks. If a plant has monitoring at all, it is often monitoring TCP, not UDP, and 2222 slips past.

The CIP Object Model

CIP is object oriented in a specific, old fashioned way. Every device exposes a set of objects, and every object is an instance of a class, and every class has attributes that can be read or written depending on the class and the service.

There are dozens of classes in the CIP specification. A few show up over and over in any real conversation about the protocol.

Identity Object (Class 0x01) is what the device says about itself. Vendor ID, device type, product code, revision, status, serial number, product name. Every EtherNet/IP device has one. It is meant to be readable by anyone on the network.

Message Router (Class 0x02) is what routes CIP messages to the correct object. It is the internal dispatcher.

Assembly Object (Class 0x04) is what defines the byte layout of an implicit I/O connection. Assemblies have fixed sizes and are what the implicit traffic on UDP 2222 is carrying.

Connection Manager (Class 0x06) is what makes and breaks connections, including the implicit I/O connections and the explicit CIP connections.

Symbol Object (Class 0x6B) is a Rockwell specific class that provides symbolic addressing, which is the thing that makes tag names work. This is the class that lets you ask a ControlLogix "read me the tag Motor_1_Run" and get an answer.

Rockwell also uses higher class numbers in the range Rockwell reserves for vendor specific extensions. The object model is extensible by design, which is the reason other vendors can add their own classes without breaking the protocol.

What a Packet Looks Like

The encapsulation header is 24 bytes. It is the same for all EtherNet/IP messages over TCP and UDP.

Command         2 bytes
Length          2 bytes
Session Handle  4 bytes
Status          4 bytes
Sender Context  8 bytes
Options         4 bytes

For a List Identity, the Command is 0x0063, the Length is zero, and everything else is zero or the sender's own identifier. The client sends this to UDP 44818, typically as a broadcast. Every EtherNet/IP device on the segment receives the broadcast, and every one of them replies.

The reply carries a CIP Identity Object. It contains:

Vendor ID           2 bytes
Device Type         2 bytes
Product Code        2 bytes
Revision            2 bytes (major and minor)
Status              2 bytes
Serial Number       4 bytes
Product Name        length prefixed string
State               1 byte

That is the entire answer to the question "who are you." Vendor ID. Family. Firmware revision. Serial number. Product name as a human readable string, something like "1756-L83E/B LOGIX5583."

Shodan has been sending List Identity requests to every reachable IPv4 address for years. It has indexed hundreds of thousands of industrial devices this way. It is not doing anything sophisticated. It is asking politely and recording who answers.

Reading Tags by Name

The protocol is not just self describing. It is semantically descriptive in a way that is unusual for an industrial protocol, and this is the part I want to slow down on.

Rockwell's engineering environment, Studio 5000, encourages engineers to give tags descriptive names. Motor_1_Run. Tank_Level_Setpoint. Valve_3_Command. Overpressure_Alarm. The names are for humans. When an engineer opens a piece of ladder logic in Studio 5000, the tag names are what they see, and the difference between Tag_00041 and Cooling_Pump_Status is the difference between a program that can be maintained and a program that cannot.

Rockwell has been teaching this practice for twenty years, because it is good engineering practice, and it is why Rockwell's ecosystem is prized by engineers who have to maintain these systems across decades.

On an EtherNet/IP network, symbolic addressing means that if you can talk CIP over TCP 44818, you can ask the controller to read the tag named Cooling_Pump_Status, and the controller will read it and send you the value. The read service is 0x4C and it takes the tag name as its payload. The write service is 0x4D and takes the tag name and a value.

There is no authentication on the tag read. There is no authentication on the tag write, in the base protocol. There is a session handle that must be established first, and there is the hardcoded key story that I will get to in a moment, and neither of those constitutes authentication in any meaningful sense.

So the attack surface, laid out plainly, is this. An attacker who can reach TCP 44818 on a Rockwell PLC can enumerate the tag names the PLC is running. The tag names are the process. They are labeled, described, and ordered in a way that reflects exactly what the process does. An attacker who has the tag names knows which one to write and what value to write.

In Modbus, the addresses are numbers. Coil 5, register 10. The attacker has to figure out what 5 and 10 mean before they can use them. In EtherNet/IP, they do not. The controller tells them.

I want to be careful not to say this is a mistake on Rockwell's part. Descriptive tag names are how you maintain a plant over thirty years. The alternative, obfuscated tag names or address only access, has an operational cost that is real and that shows up as an unmaintainable program in year fifteen. Rockwell made a tradeoff between human readability and informational exposure, and the tradeoff was correct for the environment it was designed for, and the environment it was designed for is not the environment these devices are deployed in now.

CVE-2021-22681 and the Hardcoded Key

In February 2021, researchers at Claroty disclosed a vulnerability in Rockwell's Logix controllers. The vulnerability was that Studio 5000, the engineering software, and the controllers themselves used a hardcoded cryptographic key to establish a trusted session. The key was the same across every Logix controller family and every firmware version. And it was extractable from the Studio 5000 software, which was not obfuscated in any meaningful way.

The consequence was simple. Anyone who could obtain a copy of Studio 5000, which is to say anyone who wanted to, could extract the key and use it to talk to any Logix controller as though they were a legitimate engineering workstation. The controllers would accept the session, and would then accept the reads and writes and, most consequentially, the firmware updates.

CVSS 9.8. Assigned as CVE-2021-22681.

What made the disclosure interesting was the response. Rockwell initially disputed the characterization, arguing that the authentication mechanism was working as designed and that the key was only present in the legitimate engineering software. CISA published an advisory anyway, describing the behavior as a vulnerability, and the argument went public.

Looking at it now, the disagreement was instructive because Rockwell's position, taken charitably, was a real position. The key was not "leaked." It existed in the tool that was supposed to have it. The intended access path was Studio 5000 talking to a controller. The intended user was an engineer with Studio 5000 on their workstation. Rockwell's argument was that the tool was the identity, and the tool was the customer's, and the customer was already on the network.

The counterargument, which is the one that eventually shaped the advisory and the fix, is that "the tool is the identity" is not authentication in any useful sense when the tool is installed on twenty thousand laptops around the world and the key is trivially extractable from all of them. Authentication means the identity is a secret that a specific, identifiable principal holds. When the secret is a shared constant across the entire install base, and it is present in software that the entire install base has, it is a fixed value, not a secret, and the session it establishes is not an authenticated session.

I mentioned in the Modbus piece that there is no CVE for Modbus because there is nothing to patch. EtherNet/IP is the pattern in between. There is a CVE here, and it was fixed, and the fix matters. But the technique, which is T0891 Hardcoded Credentials, appears again and again in industrial products, and the CVE was only one chapter of it. Two years after CVE-2021-22681 was patched, almost the same problem would appear in a different vendor's product line, because the design pattern that produced it was not specific to Rockwell.

The system that got patched, in other words, was a single instance of an architectural choice that the industrial sector keeps making: treat possession of the vendor's software as proof of authorization, and put the shared secret inside the software.

The Attacker Listens

Here is the part that I think earns the title of this article.

The reason EtherNet/IP has more published attack surface than Modbus is not that the protocol is more broken. It is that the protocol produces more intelligence. A passive tap on a Rockwell network, with no active scanning at all, will show an attacker:

List Identity broadcasts. Every EtherNet/IP device responds to them, and many devices broadcast their own presence on the network at periodic intervals. A twenty minute capture gives the attacker a device inventory with vendor, family, firmware version, and serial number for every EtherNet/IP device on the segment, without a single packet from the attacker.

Implicit I/O traffic on UDP 2222. The connections between PLCs and I/O blocks are all visible. From the multicast group addresses and the payload sizes, the attacker can infer which controller is running which process area, which downstream I/O is answering to which upstream controller, and the update rate for each. That is a diagram of the physical layout.

Explicit messaging on TCP 44818 from legitimate clients. An operator's HMI polling tag values, an engineering workstation connecting for a programming session, a data historian collecting values. All of this traffic is visible to anyone on the same segment. The tag names in the explicit reads are the exact names the attacker will need when they are ready to write something.

None of this requires an active scan. None of this sends a packet to the target. None of this trips an IDS that looks for network scanning, because passive listening is not scanning. An attacker with a laptop on a shared VLAN, or with a tap on a switch port, or with a route to the segment from a compromised machine on the corporate network, has all of this.

This is why the appropriate response to "should we scan the OT network" is more complicated than it looks. Active scanning on EtherNet/IP is safe in isolation. Rockwell PLCs handle List Identity requests without crashing. But active scanning produces log entries and connects to control ports, and if the attacker's goal is not to be noticed, they will not scan. They will listen, and the protocol speaks to them whether they ask or not.

The ATT&CK Mapping and the Empty CVE Column

Mapping the techniques to the framework, in the same format as the Modbus piece.

T0842 Network Sniffing. Passive capture of EtherNet/IP traffic. This is the reconnaissance. No CVE.

T0846 Remote System Discovery. Sending List Identity requests or equivalent discovery packets. Unauthenticated by design. No CVE.

T0801 Monitor Process State. Reading tags by name over explicit messaging. No CVE.

T0811 Data from Information Repositories. Enumerating the tag dictionary and the object dictionary. No CVE.

T0845 Program Upload. Using the same read services to exfiltrate the controller's running program. No CVE.

T0855 Unauthorized Command Message. Writing tags. No CVE.

T0836 Modify Parameter. Writing setpoint tags. No CVE.

T0839 Module Firmware. Uploading new firmware. CVE-2021-22681 is the well known enabler on Logix controllers, though the technique has been performed on other Rockwell families without any CVE at all.

T0891 Hardcoded Credentials. The class of technique that CVE-2021-22681 belongs to.

Read the list against the Modbus one. In Modbus, the CVE column was empty everywhere. In EtherNet/IP, it is empty almost everywhere, and where it has a value, the value describes a single vendor's particular implementation of a design pattern that the entire sector uses.

The pattern is the same as last time. The protocol layer is not where the CVEs live. The CVEs are a description of the narrow class of failures that a vendor happened to be caught on. The techniques that matter are architectural.

What to Do About It

Three things, and they are three things with a specific EtherNet/IP flavor.

Segment, and treat UDP 2222 as a first class concern. Every implicit I/O connection is a fixed multicast group and a fixed exchange rate. If you know what those are supposed to be, you can put an access control list on the port that only permits the known flows. If you do not know what they are supposed to be, that is the finding. The I/O plane should be one of the most enumerable things on the network, and in most facilities nobody has ever tried to enumerate it.

Monitor passively. Do not scan. A passive network sensor on the OT segment will capture List Identity broadcasts, discover who is asking for what, and baseline the explicit messaging flows. It will not touch a single PLC, which means it will not risk an unscheduled stop, and it will not show up in any scan detection logic the attacker might have deployed on the endpoints they control. Passive monitoring is the correct tier of visibility for a protocol that is this chatty by design.

Where CIP Security is available, use it, and where it is not, accept that "available" is the number you have. CIP Security is the ODVA extension that wraps CIP in TLS and DTLS with certificate based authentication. It has existed since around 2018. Adoption is small, and the reason is the same as with Modbus Security: the devices that most need it are the ones least able to run it, and the devices that can run it are already in a plant where a thirty year old controller is still the one making the decisions. The honest assessment is that CIP Security will be deployed on the new stuff, and the new stuff will be a fraction of the install base for the foreseeable future, and the old stuff will continue to be protected by segmentation and monitoring, which is what was always going to protect it.

And one more, which is specific to EtherNet/IP and worth saying out loud. The tag names on your controllers are a security relevant artifact. Not because you should obfuscate them, which would be a bad trade for maintenance, and would not stop an attacker who can read the traffic of a legitimate client anyway. But because the fact that they are readable to anyone on the wire should be part of the threat model. If your segmentation plan assumes that nobody outside a small set of hosts can reach TCP 44818 on a Logix controller, then in the case where that assumption is wrong, the attacker does not just have access. They have a map.

What I Did Not Solve

Two things, and the first is the one I keep circling.

I do not know how to reconcile readable tag names with a security posture that treats them as sensitive. Descriptive tag names are the only way to maintain a Rockwell system over decades. They are also the informational side of the attack surface, and there is no way to keep them out of the hands of an attacker who has already reached the network. You cannot have a controller that tells legitimate clients what its tags are and does not tell an attacker the same thing, because the protocol has no way to distinguish them. The only real answer is to keep the attacker off the network, and that answer puts all the weight on segmentation, and segmentation is the control that operators most commonly refuse because they were told it would break something.

I do not know whether CVE-2021-22681 is representative or anomalous. The Rockwell hardcoded key case is the one that got a CVE and a public fight between the vendor and CISA. Similar patterns are visible in other vendors' products without the same publicity, and I suspect the number of industrial products that effectively treat possession of a vendor tool as proof of authorization is much larger than the CVE list suggests. I do not have a way to estimate that number, and I do not trust any estimate I have seen.

Companion Project

The companion for this article is an active List Identity lab at github.com/mhebert-security/enip-lab. It has two pieces: a server that pretends to be a Rockwell ControlLogix on UDP 44818, and a client that sends the 24-byte List Identity request and prints the full reply in hex. The reply carries the vendor, the product family, the firmware revision, the serial number, and the product name, all with no authentication. Run the two together and you watch the exchange an attacker runs during discovery.
