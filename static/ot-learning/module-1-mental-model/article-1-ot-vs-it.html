Why OT Security Is Not Just IT Security with Hard Hats

Published: 2026-09-16 · Module: Module 1: The Mental Model · Code: github.com/mhebert-security/purdue-model

I spent five years as a nurse before I moved into technology, and for most of that second career I treated the two as separate things. The longer I work in security, the more that framing looks wrong. Nursing and OT security, meaning operational technology security, the discipline of securing the computers that run physical industrial processes, are built on the same logic: when the cost of a failure is irreversible, you build systems differently. A wrong dose of insulin at 3 a.m. does not wait for a rollback. A control action that opens the wrong valve does not either.

The claim of this article is that OT security is not IT security with different hardware. It is a different discipline, with a different threat model, a different risk calculus, and a different set of design constraints. The differences are structural, not cultural. Practitioners who apply IT intuitions directly end up recommending controls that OT operators correctly refuse, and the reason is usually not that the operators are behind. It is that the recommendations are wrong for the environment.

This is the technical version of that argument.

The Inversion

Every security practitioner learns the CIA triad early: confidentiality, integrity, availability, in that order. In IT, confidentiality leads. A server taken offline for two minutes to apply a security patch is an acceptable trade. Uptime is a preference. Secrecy is the priority.

In OT the order nearly reverses. Safety leads, then availability, then integrity, then confidentiality. Safety does not appear in the CIA triad at all, which tells you something about where the triad was developed.

What safety means in an ICS context, ICS meaning industrial control system, is not a vibe. It is defined by standards. IEC 61508 covers functional safety. IEC 61511 covers safety instrumented systems in the process industries. A hazard and operability study, usually shortened to HAZOP, produces the failure scenarios, and the Safety Integrity Level, or SIL, that falls out of it describes how much confidence the design is required to provide. A SIL 3 loop in a chemical plant is engineered and documented to a specific probability of failure on demand.

The system that implements that loop is the safety instrumented system, or SIS, and it is supposed to be independent of the basic process control system, which is the ordinary control system running the plant. Independent means it does not share sensors, does not share logic solvers, and does not share the network that connects them. When people say the SIS is the last line of defense, they mean that architecturally and not just aspirationally.

That has direct security consequences. If an attacker can reach both the basic process control system and the SIS through the same network path, the independence assumption is broken, and the safety analysis that justified the SIL rating no longer holds. That is a failure mode with no IT equivalent. You cannot breach your way to a safety violation on an email server.

Availability follows safety. An unexpected shutdown of an industrial process is not only a business continuity event. It can be the dangerous condition. A running compressor has thermal and pressure states that were designed around continuous operation. Stopping it abruptly is not the same as stopping it safely. The shutdown sequence is itself a control program, and it depends on the control system that is now in an unknown state.

Integrity follows availability. False sensor readings cause control decisions based on bad information. This is where attacks live. In IT, if you change data, you change a record. In OT, if you change data, a pump speeds up, a valve closes, or a breaker trips.

Confidentiality comes last. Reading historian data is a problem, especially for competitive or nation state reconnaissance, but it does not by itself cause a physical event. That is not to say confidentiality does not matter in OT. Prepositioned access depends on it. It is to say the ordering is different, and the difference matters when you are triaging.

The inversion is not a quirk of OT culture. It follows from physics. Information systems were designed to be reversible. Physical processes were not.

The Architecture, and Where It Breaks

OT systems are organized into a hierarchy called the Purdue Model, developed in the 1990s and still the dominant reference framework. Six levels, with physical machinery at the bottom, corporate network at the top, and information and commands flowing between them.

Level 0 is the physical process: sensors, actuators, valves, motors, pumps. The safety instrumented system lives here too, or is supposed to, as an independent layer that can force a safe shutdown when critical thresholds are crossed.

Level 1 is basic control. PLCs, short for programmable logic controllers, are small rugged computers running a single program in an infinite loop. Read inputs, execute logic, write outputs. Most PLCs have no user authentication on the control protocol. If you can reach a PLC on a network that speaks its protocol, you can command it. The PLC does not ask who you are.

Level 2 is supervisory control: operator HMIs and local SCADA nodes. HMI stands for human machine interface, and SCADA stands for supervisory control and data acquisition. This is the screen a human watches. Compromise here gives an attacker the ability to see what operators see, suppress alarms, display false readings, and issue commands downward, all while the screen shows normal.

Level 3 is site operations: SCADA servers, historians, engineering workstations. The engineering workstation holds the PLC project files, which are the canonical copy of the control logic running on every PLC in the facility. Whoever controls the engineering workstation controls the process.

Levels 4 and 5 are enterprise: corporate IT, email, ERP, internet connectivity.

The most important line in the architecture sits between Level 3 and Level 4. In a well designed facility, the corporate network and the control network do not communicate directly. Data flows upward through a DMZ that terminates connections on both sides. In a facility that grew by accretion, the boundary is a fiction. Remote access solutions a vendor installed to troubleshoot equipment and never removed. Historian channels that became bidirectional without anyone deciding they should be.

I have not read an incident report where the attacker invented new physics to cross that boundary. Every one of them crossed a connection that already existed.

The model itself has known gaps. The original Purdue specification assumed a single site with a hard perimeter. It does not model cloud connectivity well, or vendor remote access, or the increasingly common practice of pushing OT telemetry directly into an enterprise data lake. The various Level 3.5 DMZ patterns that integrators publish, and the looser reworkings of the model that circulate under names like Purdue 2.0, are attempts to patch it without abandoning it. Some of them are useful. None of them solve the underlying problem, which is that the physical process is now reachable from networks that were never designed to reach it.

Why Patching Is Not the Answer

The question I hear most often from IT practitioners is: if OT systems run decade old software with known CVEs, meaning Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures, the public identifiers assigned to known security flaws, why do operators not just patch?

The question is reasonable until you price the maintenance window correctly.

In IT, patching a server means scheduling a window, applying the update, rebooting, verifying services came back up. Total downtime is measured in minutes. Fallback is rolling back the patch.

In OT, patching a PLC means halting the physical process it controls. It means validating that the patch does not alter the PLC behavior in any way that affects process safety. That validation happens in a replica environment that mirrors the production configuration, which may not exist and may take months to build. The maintenance window requires operational lead time measured in weeks. After the patch goes in, every downstream sensor, actuator, and control loop gets verified. The process comes back up in a controlled sequence.

The fallback, if something breaks, is not rolling back the patch. The fallback is managing a process that is now in an unknown state, with physical consequences potentially already in motion.

So operators accept known vulnerabilities, on purpose, because the risk of the patch is measurable and the risk of the vulnerability is conditional on a network exposure they believe they have eliminated. That is a rational position when the segmentation is real. The interesting question is what happens when it is not, and the answer is that the whole risk calculus collapses.

There is a nursing analogy worth stating plainly. A nurse who waits three shifts to reach a physician about a medication order that felt wrong has done the same calculation. The risk of the intervention was harder to quantify than the risk of the status quo, and unilateral action in an unfamiliar domain carries its own liability. The difference is that nurses eventually have a way to escalate. OT operators often do not have a security team that understands what they are being asked to accept.

The Protocol Problem

This is the part the IT security mindset does not have a good analogy for.

Modbus was designed in 1979 by Modicon for direct serial communication between a master and a set of slaves on a trusted bus. It has no authentication, no integrity check beyond an optional checksum, and no encryption. None of those were considered necessary because the physical layer was the security boundary. If you could reach the bus, you were authorized to be on the bus.

DNP3, developed in the early 1990s for the electric utility industry, is standardized as IEEE 1815 and later gained a security extension called DNP3 Secure Authentication. The base protocol still runs unauthenticated by default, and the installed base heavily favors the base protocol.

PROFINET, EtherNet/IP, IEC 61850 for substation automation, OPC Classic: same pattern. These protocols were designed for industrial control networks that were, in the original threat model, isolated. They assume that any node capable of sending a well formed message is authorized to send it.

That is not a bug in the sense of a programmer making an error. It is an architectural decision that was correct for the deployment model at the time of design. The assumption was wrong from the moment facilities started connecting things for operational convenience, and the protocols cannot be retrofitted cheaply because they are the substrate of entire industries. A utility with DNP3 in its substations is not going to replace those devices next year. It is going to run them for decades.

OPC UA, the modern successor to OPC Classic, has authentication, encryption, and a proper certificate model, and it is being adopted in new installations. The installed base is measured in decades.

The practical consequence is that in an OT network the security boundary is the network. There is no identity layer to fall back on. If you are on the right VLAN, you can talk to the PLC. Segmentation is not a defense in depth nicety in OT. It is the primary control, and in a lot of environments it is the only one.

Three Advisories, Read as Architecture

The CVEs are more useful when you read them as architectural failures than as severity scores.

CVE-2021-33885, B. Braun Infusomat Space via SpaceCom. CVSS 9.8. SpaceCom is the module that connects the pump to the hospital network. The vulnerability lets anyone on that network send a command to change the drug rate, the drug concentration, or the total volume delivered. There is no login prompt. The device does not verify the origin of the command. This is the direct consequence of the design assumption above: the pump network interface was built for a trusted clinical network, and the protocol has no identity layer to fall back on.

CVE-2019-10959, BD Alaris Gateway Workstation. CVSS 10.0. The gateway manages a bank of Alaris pumps. The vulnerability allows unauthenticated firmware installation on the gateway. What makes it severe is the scaling property. One compromised gateway means every pump under its management is compromised. That is a hub and spoke failure, and the reason is structural: the gateway exists to centralize control, and centralizing control centralizes the compromise. The IT analog is a domain controller, and the analysis is structurally identical.

CVE-2015-3459, Hospira LifeCare PCA Infusion System. CVSS 10.0. The pump was designed with the assumption that it would never be on a network, and therefore did not need to authenticate network commands. When hospitals began connecting everything, the assumption broke. It is worth reading that alongside the FDA cybersecurity safety communication from the same period about the Hospira Symbiq, which told hospitals to stop using the device rather than patch it. The CVSS score does not capture what the agency concluded. The security gap was not fixable without changing what the device fundamentally was.

What these share is that none of them are exotic. They are the expected output of a design process that assumed a threat model that no longer holds. The useful question is not how the CVE happened. It is what architectural property would have prevented it, and for all three the answer is the same. An identity layer where there is currently only a network boundary, and boundaries that are enforced rather than assumed.

What a Hospital Network Actually Looks Like

Most people picture an attacker targeting one pump. That is not how the chain works.

The realistic sequence starts with a foothold on the administrative network. Phishing, a compromised vendor laptop, an unpatched VPN appliance, an insufficiently secured guest wireless network. That part is well inside the capability of commodity attackers, and hospitals are targeted constantly for exactly that access.

Then comes the pivot toward clinical networks, which is where segmentation is supposed to stop them. In many hospitals it does not, because the line between administrative and clinical was never drawn, or was drawn and then quietly deleted by a series of operational decisions. The nurses station, the EHR terminal, and the pump bank are frequently on the same VLAN. Biomedical devices and corporate laptops share address space.

Then the gateway. On the clinical network, the Alaris vulnerability requires no credentials. One firmware push and every pump the gateway manages is under attacker control. The display still shows the ordered rate. The pump executes the command it was given.

The Clinical Purdue Model

This is the healthcare equivalent, and it deserves its own treatment, so I will set it up here and take it apart in a later module.

In industrial control, the Purdue levels are backed by standards a reader can go and check. The enterprise integration levels map to IEC 62264, the international counterpart of ISA 95. The security zones and conduits that are supposed to enforce the boundaries come from IEC 62443. When somebody argues about an industrial architecture, there is a document both sides can point at.

Healthcare does not have a single numbered standard with that authority. What it has is a widely used adaptation of the same six level structure, usually called the healthcare Purdue Model or the clinical Purdue Model. Different network design guides, medical device management platforms, and consulting practices publish variants with slightly different level counts and slightly different names for each level. What they agree on is the shape.

Level 0 is the device and the patient. Infusion pumps, ventilators, physiologic monitors, imaging modalities, wearables, implantables. This is the level with no industrial equivalent, because in industry Level 0 is machinery. In healthcare it is a patient attached to machinery.

Level 1 is the device network, often a wired or wireless segment dedicated to a device class or a single vendor. Bedside networks, telemetry wireless bands, the isolated segments a radiology vendor insisted on.

Level 2 is the clinical network: the nursing unit segments, the EHR thick clients, the medication dispensing cabinets, the nurse call system, the workstations on wheels.

Level 3 is clinical data and management: the EHR servers, the PACS archive, the interface engine, and the biomedical device management platform that tracks every Level 0 device.

Level 4 is the hospital enterprise: corporate email, ERP, HR, finance, and the administrative network where the initial foothold usually lands.

Level 5 is external: health information exchanges, cloud hosted EHR components, vendor remote support paths, patient portals.

The critical boundary is the same one as in industry, sitting between Level 3 and Level 4. In a plant it separates the control network from corporate. In a hospital it separates clinical systems from enterprise IT. Both are supposed to be mediated by a DMZ that terminates connections on both sides. Both are frequently bypassed, and for the same reasons.

The other boundary that matters is one industry does not really have, sitting between Level 1 and Level 2. That is where organizational ownership usually changes. Clinical device networks are often run by Healthcare Technology Management, the department that used to be called biomedical engineering and is still staffed by biomedical equipment technicians. Level 2 and above are run by IT. Different departments, different budgets, different change control, different vocabulary, and usually neither side owns the boundary between them. The industrial version of that tension exists too, between engineering and IT, but in industry both sides at least share the vocabulary of the Purdue levels and the IEC 62443 zones. In healthcare they often do not share anything.

The nearest thing to a normative anchor on the healthcare side is IEC 80001, the standard for applying risk management to IT networks that incorporate medical devices. It does not draw the architecture. It tells you what you have to be able to demonstrate about risk when you connect a device to a network, which is a different and in some ways more useful thing. On the device side, AAMI TIR57 covers medical device information security risk management from the manufacturer and the healthcare technology management perspective. AAMI is also the professional body for the technicians who actually run Level 0 and Level 1, and the CBET certification is their credential.

What I want to write next, in a later module, is the clinical model level by level, in the same way I plan to do for the industrial one. What actually lives at each level, what crosses each boundary remotely, which vendor remote support patterns quietly dissolve the boundary, and what a defensible segmentation design looks like when the device inventory is incomplete, half the fleet is past end of support, and the only available change window is a weekend.

What I Did Not Solve

I do not have a clean answer for how to do OT security at facilities that do not have an engineering function capable of validating patches in a replica environment. Smaller water utilities, rural hospitals, and municipal infrastructure often lack the staffing or the budget for an accurate test environment. The standard advice, which is to segment, monitor, and patch with validation, assumes a capability these operators do not have.

I do not have a clean answer for the installed base. A DNP3 substation that will be in service until 2040 is not going to be replaced by OPC UA devices next year. It will run unauthenticated, on a network that must therefore be the security boundary, for the next fifteen years.

I do not have a clean answer for the boundary between safety and security in an ICS, especially when the safety instrumented system and the basic process control system share a network path. The standards say they should not. Practice in a lot of facilities says they do. Unwinding that is a capital project spanning several years, not a firewall rule.

What I do have is a clearer sense of where to look. The interesting failures in this discipline are not exotic. They are firewall rules added for operational convenience and never reviewed, remote access solutions installed by a vendor and forgotten, and patching conversations where the security team did not understand why the operations team said no.
